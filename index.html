Understanding Carpet Dirt Levels

A quick read on what the customer's carpet is actually telling you.

1
Regularly Soiled · Normal Dirty
Standard day-to-day accumulation. Vacuumed at least weekly and professionally cleaned every 6–12 months. This is routine maintenance.
2
Heavily Soiled · Really Dirty
Higher-than-normal foot traffic, or hasn't been professionally cleaned in over a year. Vacuuming may be inconsistent. Visible dirt buildup and traffic-area wear are common.
3
Severely Soiled · Extreme Cases
Not cleaned in 5+ years, extreme traffic wear, poor or nonexistent vacuuming. Buildup includes deep-set grime, odors, and staining — often requires aggressive restoration techniques.

Dirty Carpet Edges (Filtration Soiling)

Dark lines along walls or under doors? That's filtration soiling — dirty air moving through gaps and getting trapped in the carpet fibers. Usually linked to the HVAC system.

If routine cleaning doesn't resolve it, a specialized Filter Edge Treatment may be needed. Pricing varies by severity. Recommend regular HVAC filter changes to prevent it returning.
Modules / Textile ID

Textile Identification

IICRC S100 / S300 fiber ID — visual, burn, and bleach tests.
Why this matters
Wrong chemistry on the wrong fiber = damage, complaint, claim. IICRC standards require fiber ID before cleaning. Always pull samples from a hidden area — under cushions, inside seams, behind furniture, or rug fringe.

Visual & Tactile Pre-Check

First pass before any chemical work. Look and feel.

  • Sheen — natural fibers are usually matte. Synthetics often have a sheen.
  • Hand feel — wool is springy and warm. Cotton soft and cool. Polyester smooth and slick. Olefin waxy. Silk fine and slippery.
  • Construction — jute backing or hand-knotted = natural. Tufted with synthetic backing = synthetic.
  • Fiber length — short staple suggests wool or cotton. Long filament suggests synthetic.

A starting point — confirm with burn or bleach when stakes are high.

Chlorine Bleach Solubility Test

Use this when the burn test points to natural protein and you need to confirm wool before locking in your chemistry.

Procedure
  1. 1Pull a small fiber sample from a hidden area
  2. 2Drop into a small container of full-strength liquid chlorine bleach
  3. 3Wait 15–20 minutes
  4. 4Read the result below

Reading the Result

After 15–20 minutes, look at what's left in the container.

✓
Wool / Silk DISSOLVES COMPLETELY
Bleach destroys protein bonds. Treat as wool. pH 5–8 only, no chlorine, no high heat, gentle agitation, Fiber Neutralizer rinse.
—
Cotton / Cellulose STAYS INTACT
Stays intact, may discolor. Not protein.
—
Synthetics NO CHANGE
Stays intact, no change. Confirm specific synthetic via burn test.
Safety: Wear gloves. Work in ventilation. Don't mix bleach with ammonia or acid cleaners.

Modules / Spotting

Spotting Guide

Bridgepoint Spot & Stain Removal Kit · 5 categories.
Before you start
Pretest in a hidden area first. Work outside-in to prevent spreading. Don't over-apply. Tamp, don't scrub. Finish every job with Spot Stop to prevent wicking and re-soiling.
1
Synthetic Color & Dye
Kool-Aid · children's red medicine · fruit punch · sports drinks
Procedure
  1. 1Apply Red Zone Ready — enough to thoroughly wet the stain but not soak the backing
  2. 2Lay wet cotton towel (usually double thickness) over stain. Apply heated iron at lowest steam setting. Quick check every 20–30 seconds
  3. 3When color is gone, a yellow cast usually remains. Rinse thoroughly
  4. 4Mist Spot Stop and work in with the Whiz Groom
2
Organic Stains & Natural Dyes
Urine · decaffeinated coffee · mold · wood stains
Procedure
  1. 1Apply Stain Zone to contaminated area
  2. 2Lightly agitate and allow to dwell until stain is gone
  3. 3If stain is gone, extract rinse. Otherwise Stain Zone self-neutralizes with no re-soiling and continues working until it dries
3
Oil, Resin or Pigmented
Ink · grease · cosmetics · adhesives · polish · gum
Ink & Marker
  1. 1Apply Avenge Pro directly to stain, agitate, dwell 30 seconds
  2. 2Dry extract with vac tool, repeat if needed
  3. 3Apply All Solv Extreme · agitate · then Avenge Pro · agitate
  4. 4Rinse and mist with Spot Stop
Fingernail Polish

Critical containment trick: apply Avenge Pro AROUND the stain first. It blocks All Solv Extreme and dissolved pigment from wicking into the backing. Avenge Pro doesn't "set" stains — it's safe to lead with.

  1. 1Apply Avenge Pro around the contaminated area (not on it) to guard against wicking
  2. 2Apply All Solv Extreme, lightly agitate, absorb pigment into a white towel. Repeat until most pigment is gone
  3. 3Apply Avenge Pro and agitate lightly
  4. 4Rinse and mist Spot Stop, work in with Whiz Groom
Gum (chewing)
  1. 1Poke holes in gum surface with the Gum Getter
  2. 2Apply Gel Break to surface, dwell 5–10 minutes
  3. 3Agitate and remove gum with the Gum Getter into a towel. Repeat as needed. For residue: apply small amount of Gel Break, agitate with towel, then rinse thoroughly with water
  4. 4Mist Spot Stop and work in with Whiz Groom
4
Protein & Most Food Spills
Blood · egg · chocolate sauce · most food
Procedure
  1. 1Remove excess contaminant (blot, scrape, vacuum)
  2. 2Apply Avenge Pro and agitate. If hard, allow Avenge Pro to dwell and soften the contaminant
  3. 3Rinse and repeat if necessary
  4. 4Mist Spot Stop and work in with Whiz Groom
Note: Some food stains contain synthetic or organic dyes. If stain remains after the above, vacuum dry and follow Cat 1 (Red Zone Ready) for synthetic dyes or Cat 2 (Stain Zone) for organic dyes.
5
Specialty Stains
Rust · yellowing · water stains
Procedure
  1. 1Apply T-Rust
  2. 2Lightly agitate and allow to dwell until stain disappears
  3. 3Rinse thoroughly
Note: Some yellowing and water stains may also respond to Stain Zone. For unknown specialty stains, see the Unknown tab.

Unknown Stain Procedure

When the stain origin is unknown, work the kit in this order. Avenge Pro doesn't "set" stains — it's always safe to lead with.

1
Apply Avenge Pro · agitate
If stain is releasing → continue and rinse
2
If stain remains: Apply All Solv Extreme · agitate
If releasing → continue, then apply Avenge Pro, rinse
3
If stain remains: Apply Stain Zone OR Red Zone Ready
Origin is unknown → test in a hidden area to determine which works
EVERY SPOT JOB
Always
  • Pretest any solution in a hidden area before using on the visible stain
  • Remove excess (blot, scrape, vacuum) before applying liquid
  • Work from outside edges to center to prevent spreading
  • Tamp, don't scrub. Aggressive scrubbing causes texture change ("fuzzy carpet")
  • Don't over-apply — too much chemical spreads the stain and damages backing
  • Be patient. Most spotters need dwell time. Let chemistry do the work
  • Finish every job with Spot Stop — encapsulates residue, prevents re-soiling

Tile, Stone & Grout

IICRC Stone, Masonry & Ceramic Tile (SMT) training reference.
Goal: Identify the surface type correctly — the #1 rule of the SMT technician.

The Two Families of Natural Stone

Every stone job starts with this question. Get it wrong and you can destroy a floor.

Family 1

Calcareous

Made of calcium carbonate. Soft, porous, destroyed by acids — even vinegar or lemon juice etches it.

⚠ Never use acid
Marble · Limestone · Travertine · Onyx
Family 2

Siliceous

Made of silicates & quartz. Harder and generally acid-resistant, but acid cleaners still aren't recommended.

✓ Test first
Granite · Slate · Sandstone · Quartzite

Man-Made Surfaces

  • Ceramic tile — fired clay with a glaze. Durable surface; the weak point is the grout.
  • Porcelain tile — denser, less porous ceramic. Very chemical-tolerant.
  • Cementitious grout — porous like a sponge; absorbs soil and stains. Sanded vs. unsanded matters for agitation.
  • Engineered quartz — uniform speckle, resin warmth. Ruined by high heat and harsh solvents (but not by acids).
  • Cultured marble — gel-coat over resin. Treat it like plastic — never abrasives or acids.

Field ID · Natural vs. Man-Made

Six quick checks. Two answers side by side.

Pattern
Every tile is unique — veining runs THROUGH the material
Patterns repeat — find two identical tiles and it's printed
Edges & back
Same material all the way through; sawn or honed edges
Glazed face over clay body (different color under); molded back
Touch & temp
Feels cold and dense; polished stone shows crystal grain
Glaze feels glassy; resin (quartz) feels warmer, slightly plastic
Water drop
Unsealed stone darkens as it absorbs (limestone, travertine)
Glazed tile and quartz shed water — no darkening
Scratch test
Marble/limestone scratch with a knife; granite resists

Acid drop

Field tip: Not sure? A scratch test (calcareous is softer) or acid-drop test in a hidden spot tells you. Calcareous fizzes. When in doubt, treat it as calcareous — the safe assumption.

The pH Scale (Visual)

What's Actually in the Bottle

pH is only half the story. Two alkaline degreasers at the same pH can perform completely differently depending on what else is inside.

IngredientJobWhere it matters
SurfactantsLower surface tension so solution wets into pores; suspend loosened soilPorous grout & stone — also why thorough rinsing matters
Builders / alkaline saltsRaise pH, saponify fats and oilsKitchen grease, traffic film
Chelating agentsGrab hard-water minerals so they don't redepositHard-water areas; lets you clean scale without acid
SolventsDissolve oily & greasy soils chemistry alone won't touchInk, tar, adhesive — but can dull resin surfaces and epoxy grout
Oxidizers / peroxideBreak down organic color — coffee, wine, biological growthGrout stains; always test for color loss first
Read the label, not the marketing. "Safe for stone" on the front means nothing without a pH figure and a dilution ratio on the back. If a product won't tell you its pH, don't put it on someone's marble.
Safety: Never mix chemicals. Acid + bleach or ammonia + bleach creates toxic gas. Always ventilate, gloves and eye protection on, follow the SDS. High-pH strippers burn skin just like acids do.

The Pre-Job Walkthrough

  1. 1Identify the surface (Surface tab skills) and the soil type (Chemistry tab skills)
  2. 2Document existing damage — cracked tiles, missing/loose grout, chips, lippage, hollow-sounding tiles. Photograph and show the customer before starting
  3. 3Stain vs. etch — a stain is IN the stone (absorbed, darker). An etch is damage TO the stone (acid burn, dull/lighter). Cleaning removes stains; etching needs honing/polishing
  4. 4Test in an inconspicuous area — chemical, temperature, and pressure. Every time. No exceptions
  5. 5Set expectations — permanently stained grout may need colorant, not just cleaning. Say it up front

Stain vs. Etch

Different problems. Different fixes. Know the difference before you promise anything.

Problem 1

Stain

Something in the stone. Absorbed. Usually appears darker. Comes from spills, food, oils, urine.
Fix: clean or poultice
Problem 2

Etch

Damage to the stone. Appears lighter, dull ring or spot. Caused by acid contact — vinegar, wine, citrus, soft drinks.
Fix: honing / polishing
Remember: If you clean over pre-existing damage without documenting it, YOU own that damage in the customer's eyes. Photos + walkthrough = protection.

The SMT Six-Step Sequence

Same order. Every job.

1
Dry Soil Removal — vacuum / sweep
Wet cleaning over grit = mud + scratches
2
Apply Solution — correct chemistry, correct dilution
Follow label dilution — more is NOT better
3
Dwell — 5 to 15 minutes, keep wet
Chemistry needs time to break soil bonds. Skipping dwell is the #1 rookie mistake
4
Agitate — rotary brush or grout brush
Mechanical action lifts soil from the pores
5
Rinse & Extract — hot water extraction or spinner tool
Removes soil AND residue. Repeat until water runs clear
6
Dry & Inspect — air movers, final walkthrough
Prevents slips; confirms results with the customer

C.H.A.T. — The Four Dials You Control

Every job balances four factors. Turn one down and you have to turn another up. This is what separates a technician from someone pushing a machine.

C
Chemical
Breaks the bond between soil and surface
Turn down for: delicate stone, sealed surfaces, food-prep areas — compensate with time and agitation
H
Heat
Speeds the reaction — roughly doubles activity every 18°F
Turn down for: waxes & coatings (they soften), resin surfaces, radiant-heated floors
A
Agitation
Mechanically lifts soil out of pores and texture
Turn down for: soft grout, polished stone, cleft slate with loose flakes
T
Time
Dwell — lets chemistry finish the work
Rarely turn down. Time is the free dial, and it's the one rookies skip
Use it as a decision tool. Polished marble means C and A both come down — so H and T go up. Restaurant quarry tile can take all four wide open. Say the trade-off out loud before you start.

How Do You Know the Rinse Is Done?

  • Rinse water comes back clear, not tinted or foamy
  • Wipe a dry white towel across a dried section — it should come up clean, no film
  • On alkaline jobs, a pH strip in the rinse water reading near neutral tells you the chemistry is out
  • A haze appearing as the floor dries is residue, not soil. Rinse again — don't reach for more product
Equipment & safety: Cap pressure on stone and soft grout (high PSI blows out grout). Contain overspray, mask baseboards and cabinets, post wet-floor signs. PPE: gloves, eye protection, slip-resistant boots.

How Grout Is Installed

Why it matters to cleaners.

  • Mix & slake — cementitious grout is mixed, rested (~10 min), remixed. Too much water = weak, powdery grout that crumbles under aggressive cleaning
  • Float & pack — pressed diagonally into joints with a rubber float. Poorly packed joints leave voids — they trap soil and blow out under high pressure
  • Sponge & haze — excess sponged off; leftover film is grout haze. Cement haze on ceramic can take a mild acid haze remover — never on marble
  • Cure — cement grout cures ~28 days to full strength. Light cleaning only for the first 48–72 hrs. Sealer per manufacturer
  • Why you care — crumbling grout = installation or water problem, not a cleaning problem. Document it and quote a re-grout or colorant instead of over-scrubbing
Field check: Drop water on the grout. Darkens fast → porous cementitious, unsealed → needs sealing after cleaning. Beads up → sealed, epoxy, or urethane → cleaning only, skip the sealer pitch.

Etch Repair — Honing & Polishing

Cleaning never fixes an etch. The stone surface is physically dissolved — remove material until the damage is gone, then rebuild the finish.

Light etch
water spots · polished marble
Marble polishing powder + white pad, low speed, water
Gloss restored in minutes; spot repair blends
Moderate etch
dull traffic lanes
Hone with fine diamonds (400 → 800 → 1500 → 3000), then polish
Full section restored — blend to a grout line or wall
Deep etch
scratches · lippage
Grind with coarse diamonds (50 → 100 → 200) then work up the grit chain
Restoration job, not a cleaning job — price it separately
Grit rule: never skip more than one grit step. Each pass only removes the scratch pattern of the pass before it — jumping grits leaves a haze you cannot polish out, and you end up starting over.

Stain Removal — The Poultice

An absorbent powder + the right chemical mixed to peanut-butter paste. It sits on the stain and pulls it back out of the stone as it dries.

Match the chemical to the stain
  • Oil / grease → degreaser or acetone
  • Organic (coffee, wine, leaf) → hydrogen peroxide
  • Ink / dye → solvent
  • Biological growth → peroxide-based cleaner
Method
  1. 1Pre-wet the stone with water
  2. 2Apply paste about ¼" thick and 1" past the stain edge
  3. 3Cover with plastic, tape the edges, dwell 24–48 hours
  4. 4Uncover and let dry completely — the drying is what lifts the stain
  5. 5Scrape off, rinse, repeat if the stain lightened but didn't clear
Expectations: deep old stains often take three or four cycles, and some never leave completely. Say so up front.
Careful: most rust removers are acidic. On marble, limestone, or travertine they will etch while they work on the rust — you trade a stain for permanent damage. Rust in calcareous stone is usually a restoration referral, not a cleaning task.

Efflorescence

White powdery bloom on grout, pavers, and stone. It's soluble mineral salt — moisture travels through the assembly, dissolves salts, carries them to the surface, and leaves them as the water evaporates. Moisture symptom, not soil.

  • Remove it dry first — vacuum and brush. Wetting it just redissolves and redeposits
  • Residual haze on acid-tolerant surfaces can take a diluted acidic cleaner, followed by a thorough neutral rinse. Never on calcareous stone
  • If you don't stop the moisture — leaking irrigation, no vapor barrier, grade against the building — it comes straight back. Say so before you seal anything over it

Slip Resistance — Your Biggest Liability After the Truck Leaves

  • Residue is slippery. Incomplete rinsing leaves surfactant film that gets dangerous the first time it's wet again
  • Industry benchmark for level interior floors expected to get wet is a wet dynamic coefficient of friction (DCOF) of 0.42 or higher under ANSI A326.3. Polished stone and topical gloss coatings can fall below it
  • Adding a high-gloss coating to a commercial floor without a traction additive can turn a clean floor into a claim. Ask what the space is used for before you propose gloss
  • Wet-floor signs stay up until you've confirmed the floor is dry — not until you're done cleaning
0.42 The DCOF standard. ANSI A326.3 minimum for wet interior floors. Below this and the floor is legally slippery.

Movement Joints, Caulk & Hollow Tile

  • Every change of plane — wall to floor, inside corners, tub and counter edges — should be flexible sealant, not grout. Cracked grout in a corner is a design failure, not dirt
  • Cracking in a straight line across a floor usually means a missing movement joint or a moving substrate. Cleaning won't change it — aggressive cleaning will widen it
  • Tap-test suspect areas. A hollow sound means the tile has debonded from the substrate. Note it, photograph it, avoid it — pressure will pop it loose and it becomes your tile
  • Mold in a shower: black growth on the surface of silicone usually means the sealant is colonized underneath and needs replacing. Bleach lightens the color and leaves the problem
The professional move: pre-existing conditions are a sales opportunity, not a problem to hide. "I found three things I want to show you before I start" builds more trust than a perfect-looking invoice ever will — and it puts repair work on the estimate.
